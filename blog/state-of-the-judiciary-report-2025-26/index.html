The State of the Judiciary Report 2025/26

A Welcome First Step, Not Yet a Destination

State of the Judiciary and the Administration of Justice Report 2025/2026

JACQUELINE WANJIKU WAIHENYA, ADVOCATE

To: Members of the Law Society of Kenya, Court Users and the General Public

Dear Colleagues, Court Users and Fellow Kenyans,

Introduction

I write to you as a practising advocate who appears before our courts, seeking to serve you and advance sustainable justice. My subject is the State of the Judiciary and the Administration of Justice Report for the financial year 2025/2026 (the Report), which the Chief Justice has presented to Parliament and to the people of Kenya. In her Foreword, the Chief Justice describes the Report as an important instrument of public accountability through which the Judiciary accounts for the judicial authority entrusted to it by the people. I agree with that description, and in this letter I take it at its word.

The Report contains, at Appendix 2.15, a Judges and Judicial Officers Individual Performance Report. It sets out the recorded output of 119 High Court Judges, 61 Environment and Land Court Judges, 21 Employment and Labour Relations Court Judges, 619 Magistrates, 45 Kadhis and 187 Small Claims Court Adjudicators, officer by officer and station by station. I welcome this. It is a meaningful first step and the opening of a long overdue conversation on judicial accountability. It did not come about by accident. It follows years of sustained pressure from the Bar, from litigants and from civil society for the Judiciary to show the public, in concrete terms, how the judicial authority of the people is being exercised. The Chief Justice deserves credit for taking that step, and the Bar deserves credit for insisting on it.

The step has not been free of controversy. On 19 November 2026, the High Court temporarily barred publication of individual performance data. The Court discharged that order on 20 November 2026. I respect the concerns of colleagues on the Bench, and I return to them below. But I have argued before, including in the Lenaola v Havi matter, that the public has a legitimate interest in scrutinising how public officers, including judges, exercise their responsibilities. That scrutiny must

respect judicial independence and be fair to the officers concerned.

Publication alone, however, is no cause for celebration. A report is a mirror, not a remedy. The real test lies in what follows. Who takes responsibility for the shortcomings the Report identifies? What corrective action will be required, and of whom? And when will advocates and litigants experience measurable, tangible improvement in the courts where they spend their days? The Report itself anticipates these questions. The Chief Justice cautions that aggregate clearance rates alone cannot define success. The Deputy Chief Justice states that the objective is not to pursue numerical targets for their own sake but to understand what the evidence reveals and to act upon it. I write to hold the institution to those words, and to offer practical proposals for doing so.

What the Report Gets Right

The headline figures deserve recognition. During the year, 561,580 cases were filed and 576,702 were resolved, a Case Clearance Rate of 103 per cent and the second consecutive year in which the courts resolved more cases than were filed. The pending caseload fell by 2.5 per cent, from an adjusted opening balance of 595,076 to 579,954 (Table 2.2:1). Backlog older than one year fell by 15.8 per cent, from 405,486 to 341,412 cases. One in three cases resolved during the year, 190,779 in all, was already more than a year old, and 37,757 of them had been pending for more than five years. Behind those numbers are litigants whose cases were brought to a close. That is real work by real judicial officers.

The Report is also candid about the conditions in which that work is done. As at 30 June 2026, only 241 of 407 established Judge positions (59 per cent) and 637 of 1,200 Magistrate positions (53 per cent) were filled. Only 11 per cent of Registrar and Deputy Registrar positions and 38 per cent of Legal Researcher positions were filled. On average, each Magistrate carried about 694 pending cases and each High Court Judge about 602. The Court of Appeal had 27 Judges against a statutory maximum of 70 before 15 appointments raised its

Appendix 2.15 is, to its credit, careful about how it should be read. It states that it is not a ranking, and it deliberately keeps officers in the order in which records were extracted from the Case Tracking System so that it is not read as one. It flags newly appointed Judges, officers who were elevated or promoted mid-year, Heads of Station, Deputy Registrars and Adjudicators. It lists the other stations at which each Judge’s work was recorded through Rapid Results Initiatives, service weeks and Mahakama Popote. It openly concedes that quality of judgments and case-weighted workload are outside its scope. That honesty is valuable, and it is the right foundation on which to build.

Finally, Chapter 9 of the Report is unusually self-critical. It acknowledges inconsistencies in caseload data and unclear institutional ownership of reported statistics. It recommends quarterly reconciliation of records, a common data dictionary, sign-off of statistics by Heads of Station and Registrars, and independent quality assurance. It calls for case age and case weighting to be integrated into station performance targets, and for a diagnostic review of the decline in the institutional performance score from 97.67 per cent in FY 2022/23 to 95.29 per cent and then 88.12 per cent in FY 2024/25. The Report also records work on a Case Weighting Framework. The direction of travel is right. My concern is the pace, and whether the follow-through will be enforceable.

Critical Weaknesses of the Report

Workload, complexity and resources

The individual schedules present raw output without the denominators that would make it meaningful. A Magistrate at Milimani Commercial Courts works at a station carrying 35,876 pending cases, 24,931 of them in backlog. A colleague at Tinderet works at a station with 187 pending cases. Their recorded totals cannot fairly be compared, yet the schedules do not show, for each officer, the files allocated, the age of the inherited docket, the mix of case types, the number of sitting days or the support staff available. The same problem appears in the superior courts. The average time to dispose of a civil matter was about 3,418 days at Eldoret High Court and about 2,454 days in the Milimani Family Division, against about 88 days at Kibera High Court.

Station data sometimes points in the opposite direction from individual data. The Milimani High Court Civil Appellate Division, created during the year to absorb civil appeals, many of them arising from Small Claims

Court decisions, recorded a clearance rate of only 61 per cent. Its pending caseload rose from 2,746 to 3,787. Yet its six Judges together recorded 2,729 outputs. The clearance rate counts concluded cases, while the output total includes interlocutory rulings. These figures alone cannot establish why the division’s pending caseload grew (section 2.7 and Appendix 2.15). The Report also records that, because Registrars and Legal Researchers are so thinly staffed, Judges and Magistrates spend more of their time on case administration, research and drafting that support staff would otherwise do. None of that work appears in an individual output count.

Quantity is not quality

The Report is explicit that it records output volumes only. Its final column is the arithmetic sum of judgments, rulings closing a case, interlocutory rulings and other resolutions. No category is weighted. A consent order adopted in minutes counts the same as a reasoned constitutional judgment written over months. The composition of the totals shows why this matters. In the Magistrates’ Courts, other resolutions account for 324,470 of 473,287 recorded outputs, about 69 per cent. In the Kadhis’ Courts they account for 45,390 of 48,493, about 94 per cent. In the Small Claims Court the figure is about 56 per cent, and in the High Court about 48 per cent. Other resolutions include withdrawals, sentencing on a plea of guilty, consents, uncontested grants and consolidation of files.

The effect is visible within single stations. At the Mombasa Law Courts, one Magistrate recorded 2,697 outputs, of which 2,651 were other resolutions and 23 were judgments. A colleague at the same station recorded 2,024 outputs, including 549 judgments. I draw no conclusion about either officer, and that is precisely the point: the totals invite a conclusion the data cannot support. Interlocutory rulings raise a similar difficulty. The Report notes that a single case may generate several interlocutory rulings before final determination, and each is counted. A file that stalls through repeated applications may therefore add more to an officer’s total than a file that is heard and decided promptly.

What litigants care about is the correctness, clarity and timeliness of reasoned decisions. The Report’s own complaints data confirms this. Of the complaints received, 279 (about 22 per cent) concerned the merits of decisions or the exercise of judicial discretion, 151 concerned adjournments and delayed trials, and 67 concerned delayed judgments and rulings. Of the 259 petitions before the Judicial Service Commission against Judges, 55 alleged incompetence, including entertaining matters without jurisdiction and issuing conflicting rulings. Yet the Report contains no indicator of reserved-judgment delay, appellate outcomes, peer assessment of reasoning or court-user satisfaction at the level of the individual officer.

Missing contextual qualifiers

The interpretive cautions sit in explanatory notes, not alongside each officer’s figures. Apart from markers for new appointees, Heads of Station and Deputy Registrars, a reader cannot see an officer’s leave, secondments, committee work or periods away on special benches. The Report concedes that leadership and committee work takes considerable judicial time and has not been quantified. The Court of Appeal schedules illustrate the risk. One Judge of Appeal is shown with four single-judge outputs for the year. Read alone, that figure misleads. The same Judge sat on a Nyeri bench that delivered 72 determinations and chairs the Judicial Service Superannuation Scheme. The Supreme Court, for its part, is reported only as a single collegial bench with 92 determinations. The Report’s justification is principled, but the public will notice that the most junior officers are disaggregated while the apex court is not.

Transparency in collection, verification and presentation

All individual data was extracted from the Case Tracking System, the same system whose records required the opening pending caseload to be reduced by 3,552 cases that had already been resolved. The Report also contains figures that do not reconcile with one another. The reported reductions of 2.5 per cent and 3.1 per cent use different starting points. The first uses the adjusted opening balance of 595,076 and the second uses the previously reported balance of 598,628 (pp. 22 and 27). The Supreme Court is said to have resolved 74 matters at a clearance rate of 99 per cent, and elsewhere 77 matters at about 103 per cent. One account reports 1,243 public complaints, of which 1,111 were resolved and 132 remained pending. Another reports 1,238 new complaints and 120 carried forward, with 1,284 resolved and 74 pending. The report does not reconcile these two accounts (pp. 133–135 and 178). The number of constituencies without a court is given as 101 and, in the appendix, as 104 with no court of any kind. Chapter 2 records 11,650 mediation referrals and 6,640 agreements, including full agreements, partial agreements and consents. Chapter 9, issue 12, refers to 11,360 referrals and approximately 4,000 settlements. The report leaves it unclear whether the two accounts cover the same sets of cases and categories of outcome (pp. 42–43 and 172).

If aggregate figures differ within one document, the public is entitled to ask how robust the individual figures are. The Report does not say whether officers were given an opportunity to verify their own data before publication, how disputed entries were resolved, or whether any independent body audited the extraction. The Report explains that station totals count concluded files once, including work done through initiatives such as Mahakama Popote. Individual totals count recorded outputs, including interlocutory rulings and work

undertaken across stations (section 2.7 and Appendix 2.15). That distinction should be made clear wherever the figures are published. Finally, the institutional performance evaluation reported is for FY 2024/25, not the year under review, and the staff appraisal results show a mean score of 94.4 per cent, with only 14 of 5,620 appraised staff rated outstanding. In FY 2024/25, 80.6 per cent of appraised staff were rated Good. That concentration warrants a closer look at how the appraisal distinguishes performance across roles. The Judiciary-wide backlog, by itself, cannot establish whether individual staff ratings were justified (p. 138).

The risk of perverse incentives

Readers may treat published totals as scores despite the report’s cautions. That creates a risk of favouring outputs that count quickly: withdrawals, consents, dismissals for want of prosecution and interlocutory rulings, and to defer the long, difficult hearing. The Report itself shows how a clearance rate can rise for reasons unrelated to the quality of justice. The Small Claims Court recorded the highest clearance rate, 120 per cent, in a year in which its filings fell by about 62 per cent, from 158,357 to 60,155, and in which many claims by unlicensed digital lenders were not sustained and matters outside its jurisdiction were transferred out. Chapter 9 rightly warns that an aggregate clearance rate above 100 per cent can coexist with prolonged waits for litigants. In the Magistrates’ Courts, Table 2.2:2 records 403,550 resolutions against 399,922 filings, a difference of 3,628. The narrative on p. 27 gives a higher margin of about 5,200. Even on that higher figure, the Report acknowledges that clearing a backlog of 274,177 cases at that pace would take decades. Metrics that reward volume over age will not change that.

What Needs to Change: Recommendations for Future Reports

Performance data should be contextualised. For each officer, future reports should state the opening and closing docket, its age profile and case-type mix, the number of sitting days, the support staff ratio at the station, leadership and committee responsibilities, and authorised absence expressed in days. Station infrastructure and resource indicators, such as courtroom availability and connectivity, should sit alongside. The Case Weighting Framework should be finalised, its methodology published for comment, and then applied, so that a complex land suit and a traffic plea are no longer counted alike.

Qualitative indicators should be introduced alongside quantitative ones. These should include the time between the close of hearing and delivery of judgment, compliance with delivery timelines, the outcomes of appeals from an officer’s decisions read with appropriate

caution, structured peer review of a random sample of judgments by experienced reviewers working with the Kenya Judiciary Academy, and periodic surveys of court users and advocates. Adjournments should be attributed to their cause, so that court-driven delay is distinguished from delay caused by parties, witnesses or, candidly, advocates who are not ready. The Report identifies advocates not being ready as one of the leading causes of the 274,478 adjournments recorded. The Bar must own its share.

Data integrity requires verification and audit. The Chapter 9 recommendations on quarterly reconciliation, a common data dictionary, sign-off by Heads of Station and independent quality assurance should be implemented before the next report, with progress published. Every officer should receive a pre-publication statement of their figures with a fixed period to query them. An independent statistical or audit body should certify the extraction methodology.

The users of the courts should help design the measures. The Judiciary should establish a standing technical working group, including the Law Society of Kenya, Court Users Committees, academia and civil society, to review performance metrics before each reporting cycle. Bench and Bar committees at station level are well placed to test whether the figures match the experience of the people who use those courts.

Data should be disaggregated to permit meaningful comparison. Outputs should be broken down by case type, age band and mode of disposal, and judgments on the merits should be reported separately from other resolutions. Comparisons should be made only within genuine peer groups: officers of the same court and rank, at stations of similar load, with full-year service. The underlying data should be released in machine-readable form so that the profession and researchers can analyse it responsibly.

My Proposal: Mandatory Periodic Competency Reviews for Judges and Judicial Officers

Better reports are necessary, but they are not sufficient. Data tells us where to look. It does not tell us what to do. If elected, I will champion a structured and enforceable system of Mandatory Periodic Competency Reviews for Judges and Judicial Officers, designed to strengthen professional standards, accountability and the quality of justice delivery. The first step is to make the existing appraisal framework produce clear and enforceable follow-through. The proposal rests on five pillars.

Independent assessment. Each officer’s work should be evaluated regularly and objectively, at defined intervals, by panels independent of the officer’s immediate

supervisors. Assessment should look at the officer’s actual work, including a sample of judgments and rulings, case-management records and timeliness, and not merely at output counts. Published performance data would be the starting point for that assessment, never its verdict.

Targeted capacity building. Where gaps in competence or professional skill are identified, the officer should receive remedial training designed for that gap. The Kenya Judiciary Academy ran 22 programmes during the year, but the Report records that budget constraints limited the scope and frequency of training, and that only 4.8 per cent of judicial staff were trained. Generic seminars cannot close individual gaps. The Kenya Judiciary Academy Bill, which the Report asks Parliament to fast-track, offers an opportunity to give the Academy a clear mandate and secure funding for individual development plans.

Reassessment. Training should be followed, within a fixed period, by reassessment to confirm whether the identified gaps have been addressed. Without reassessment, capacity building becomes attendance, and attendance is not improvement.

Accountability. Where persistent incompetence or professional misconduct is established, it must attract appropriate action in accordance with the Constitution, the Judicial Service Act and due process. The Judiciary already has an appraisal framework. My proposal is to require targeted support, a fixed reassessment date and documented follow-through where that framework identifies a competency gap. Of the 168 petitions against Judges concluded during the year, 107 were dismissed at preliminary evaluation, 45 after the Judge’s response and two after a hearing, and 14 were withdrawn. The Report identifies two principal reasons for dismissal. Some petitions challenged the merits of decisions, which belong in appeal or review proceedings. Others provided insufficient evidence to establish grounds for removal (p. 136). That is correct. Where independently verified evidence points to a recurring competency concern, the appraisal framework should provide for a fair assessment and an opportunity to respond. A dismissed petition does not establish a competency gap.

Effective performance appraisals. The existing appraisal framework must translate its findings into practical action, measurable improvement and accountability. The Report itself observes that evidence from integrity and justice-sector assessments is not yet consistently translated into tracked operational improvements, and recommends a common action register with responsible officers, deadlines and outcome indicators. I would press for that register to cover individual appraisal outcomes too, and for the Judicial Service Commission to receive and review it.

Together, these pillars bridge the gap between where we are and where we must be. The Report gives us data on

Judicial Independence and Accountability: Holding the Balance

I am conscious that some colleagues on the Bench will read any proposal for competency review as a threat to judicial independence. I take that concern seriously. Independence under Article 160 of the Constitution is not a privilege of judges. It is a guarantee to the people that their disputes will be decided without fear, favour or outside pressure. But independence has never meant immunity from evaluation. It protects how a judge decides. It does not exempt the institution from ensuring that those who decide are competent, diligent and fit for office.

The line the Commission must not cross is clear. No competency review should assess whether a judge reached the right outcome in a particular case, and no officer should ever be penalised for the substance of a decision. Correction of outcomes belongs to appeal and review. The Commission has itself applied this principle in dismissing petitions that challenged the merits of decisions. A competency review should examine craft, not conclusions: the quality of legal reasoning and research, the management of the docket, timeliness, judicial temperament and adherence to the Code of Conduct.

The safeguards follow naturally. The criteria should be published in advance. Reviewers should be insulated from the Executive and from political influence. The officer must have a full right to respond. Individual review outcomes should remain confidential unless and until due process establishes a matter that requires disciplinary action. Nothing in the system should touch a pending case. For superior court judges, any removal proceedings would follow Article 168 of the Constitution. Designed in this way, a competency review protects judges. It replaces crude rankings and trial by social media with a fair, informed and confidential assessment. As Justice Robert Jackson of the United States Supreme Court once observed, the courts are not final because they are infallible, but infallible only because they are final. Finality is tolerable to the public only when it is earned through competence, and an

accountable Judiciary is a stronger, not a weaker, Judiciary.

Call to Action

I invite every member of the Law Society of Kenya to read Appendix 2.15 for themselves, not to rank judges or to settle scores, but to test the figures against what they see in court. Ask whether the numbers reflect the dockets, the delays and the adjournments you know. Then write to me with your observations, and raise them in your branches and in your Bench and Bar committees. The Deputy Chief Justice has invited advocates to engage systematically and constructively with the Report. Let us accept that invitation in full.

I invite litigants and court users, who carry the heaviest cost of delay, to share their experience of adjourned hearings, missing files and long-awaited judgments. I invite civil society, academia and the media to scrutinise the data with rigour and fairness. And I invite the Judiciary and the Judicial Service Commission to commit publicly to a timetable for reconciling the discrepancies in this Report, publishing a methodology and data dictionary, finalising case weighting, and consulting the Bar on the design of next year’s report.

Finally, I aspire to represent you as the Law Society of Kenya’s Female Representative to the Judicial Service Commission. I offer a clear agenda: fair and contextual performance data, independent competency review, targeted capacity building and accountability that respects independence. The Report is a starting point. With your support, I will work to make sure it is not the end of the story.

Yours faithfully, JACQUELINE WANJIKU WAIHENYA Advocate of the High Court of Kenya
