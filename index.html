The State of the Judiciary Report 2025/26

A Welcome First Step, Not Yet a Destination

State of the Judiciary and the Administration of Justice Report 2025/2026

JACQUELINE WANJIKU WAIHENYA, ADVOCATE

To: Members of the Law Society of Kenya, Court Users and the General Public

Dear Colleagues, Court Users and Fellow Kenyans,

Introduction

I write to you as a practising advocate who appears before our courts, seeking to serve you and advance sustainable justice. My subject is the State of the Judiciary and the Administration of Justice Report for the financial year 2025/2026 (the Report), which the Chief Justice has presented to Parliament and to the people of Kenya.

The Report contains, at Appendix 2.15, a Judges and Judicial Officers Individual Performance Report. It sets out the recorded output of 119 High Court Judges, 61 Environment and Land Court Judges, 21 Employment and Labour Relations Court Judges, 619 Magistrates, 45 Kadhis and 187 Small Claims Court Adjudicators, officer by officer and station by station. I welcome this.

WAIHENYA TIDBITS / ACCESS TO JUSTICE

Justice people can access

The everyday work of making rights meaningful

WAIHENYA TIDBITS

Access to justice begins with a simple question: can a person understand where to go, what to expect and how to be heard? A legal right matters, but its promise is harder to realise when the route to a remedy is confusing, costly or out of reach.

The experience of justice is shaped by ordinary encounters. Clear directions, accessible information, respectful communication and reasons that people can understand all help make institutions more approachable. These are not substitutes for sound decisions; they are part of the conditions that allow people to participate in the process.

WAIHENYA TIDBITS / LEADERSHIP

Leadership that earns trust

Accountability as a daily practice

WAIHENYA TIDBITS

Trust in an institution is built through repeated choices: how leaders explain decisions, how they respond to questions and whether they follow through on their commitments. A strong statement of values is a beginning. The harder work is making those values visible in practice.

Accountable leadership welcomes scrutiny. It sets clear responsibilities, shares reasons for decisions and creates room for concerns to be raised without fear. This does not mean that every decision will satisfy everyone. It means that the process can be examined and that mistakes can be acknowledged and addressed.

WAIHENYA TIDBITS / DISPUTE RESOLUTION

Mediation and the room for resolution

Making space for dialogue without losing sight of fairness

WAIHENYA TIDBITS

A dispute is rarely only a disagreement about a legal position. It may also involve a relationship, a business, a family or a community trying to find a way forward. Mediation can create space for those affected to discuss the problem and explore options together.

The process does not ask parties to abandon their rights. It offers a setting in which they can identify interests, test possible solutions and decide whether an agreement is acceptable. A mediator supports the conversation; the parties remain responsible for their own decisions.

WAIHENYA TIDBITS / LAW & TECHNOLOGY

Technology and human judgment

Keeping people at the centre of a changing justice system

WAIHENYA TIDBITS

Technology is changing how people find information, manage work and communicate with institutions. In justice, it can help make services easier to navigate and reduce repetitive administrative work. Its value, however, depends on the problem it solves and the people it serves.

A useful starting point is to ask what court users and practitioners need. A new tool may help someone locate a form or follow the progress of a matter, but it should be designed around a real need rather than adopted simply because it is available.
